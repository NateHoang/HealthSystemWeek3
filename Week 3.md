## Page 13

As is the case in our general healthcare systems, in oral healthcare the vast majority of money is spent providing health care to repair existing disease, with minimal expenditures on all of the other social determinants of health although these are known to have a significant impact on oral health.

## Page 14

1. And, to an even greater degree than in general health systems, the vast majority of money in the oral health systems is spent on repairing or managing the effect or oral disease as opposed to addressing the social determinants of oral disease.

## Page 15

1. As is the also the case in our general healthcare systems, the structure of the business models for delivering oral health care, again, referred to as the Commercial Determinant of Health, have a significant impact on how gets oral health care and who has oral disease vs oral heath. Oral health in the United States in primarily delivered by privately owned business where making money is a primary goal of the business.

## Page 18

1. These disparities also create significant issues for dental practices. This data shows what is referred to as the “procedure mix” for dental practices, which refers to the number of different kinds of procedures that are performed on average. In 1959, the procedure most commonly performed was amalgam restorations. Amalgam was the dominant restorative material used at that time, so this represents a measure of the number of restorations performed in dental practices. By 2005 there had been a significant shift with restorations accounting for only 14% of the procedure mix and three quarters of the procedures performed being diagnostic or preventive procedures.

## Page 19

1. More recent data verifies that this trend has continued. This chart shows the procedure mix in both private and Medicaid populations, with the results being roughly the same. Again, about three quarters of the procedures performed in dental practices are now diagnostic or preventive procedures.
2. This raises a question. What happened to all of the restorative procedures?

## Page 20

1. An additional piece of data to help understand this change in the procedure mix of dental practices is to realize that dental care has gotten a lot more expensive for most people than it used to be. This data from the Bureau of Labor Statistics shows the consumer price index, in green, which is a measure of the general increase in prices across many types of goods and services. The general CPI has grown much more slowly than the CPI for dental services, in red. Stated the other way around, the CPI for dental services, a measure of the average cost of dental services for the average person, has risen much faster than the general CPI. This has made dental care unaffordable for many people. In fact, surveys of why people put off or do not have dental care indicate that the reason most often cited is that dental care is too expensive.

## Page 21

1. As can be seen in this slide, the rate of increase in the consumer price index for dental services has also risen much faster than the rate of increase for physician services.

## Page 22

1. Numerous studies and surveys have determined that people are much more likely to put off having dental care based on the “price” of dental services than would put off having other aspects of health care due to the price of that care.
2. Why do you think that is the case?

## Page 23

1. This chart presents some data showing that people tend to not obtain needed dental care because of cost at a far higher rare than those who do not obtain other forms of health care due to cost. This is a long-term trend, and the gap is getting wider. Again, the question is why? Why is dental care more sensitive to price than other aspects of health care?

## Page 24

1. The primary reason is that people have to pay for oral health expenses out-of-pocket to a much higher degree than other health services. This chart shows that out-of-pocket payment for dental services was second only to out-of-pocket payment for prescription drugs. This is even the case for people who have dental “insurance” since dental insurance plans are really defined-benefit plans with annual maximum coverage limits. These limits have not risen nearly as fast as the price of dental care, so even people with dental insurance need to pay out-of-pocket for many procedures.

## Page 25

1. One conclusion from all the data we have presented is that we have a current dental care system that primarily serves the wealthiest and healthiest segments of the population. While this is not exclusively true, it is clear that those most likely to access dental care in dental offices and clinics, are people with higher family incomes, those who are best educated, and those who tend have the least amount of disease. At the same time, people from low income and other marginalized populations are less likely to seek dental care in dental offices and clinics and have the most disease, much of which goes untreated.
